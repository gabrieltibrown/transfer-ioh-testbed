Operating Under Pressure: 
Scaling a Stream-Processing Pipeline for Real-Time Intraoperative Hypotension Prediction

Gabriel Brown
M.Sc. Data Science, Freie Universität Berlin - Master’s Thesis Proposal
TRANSFER Project, Charité - Universitätsmedizin Berlin
1. Introduction
The TRANSFER project aims to develop real-time clinical decision support from high-frequency intraoperative monitoring data. Doing so requires not only accurate prediction models, but also streaming infrastructure capable of delivering predictions reliably and within the time available for clinical action. This thesis studies that infrastructure problem using intraoperative hypotension (IOH) prediction as a concrete use case.
IOH is associated with poor postoperative outcomes, with risk increasing with the depth and duration of mean arterial pressure below 65 mmHg. Models can predict IOH minutes in advance, but a prediction is useful only if it reaches the anaesthetist in time to act. The available warning time is shared by data processing, alert confirmation, clinician response and treatment. This thesis focuses on the part engineering can directly reduce: pipeline delay.
Perioperative monitoring makes this a systems problem. A single monitor emits numerics at 1–5 Hz and waveforms at 100–200 Hz, with ECG at 500 Hz, across a median of six waveform and ten numeric channels (Joachim et al. 2026). Many such cases must share infrastructure without one case impeding another.
Joachim et al. (2026) describe a closely related platform built around Philips DataWarehouse Connect, Kafka and Flink, deployed across 77 operating-room and ICU beds. Its evaluation, however, reports coverage rather than end-to-end latency or scaling under concurrent load. Comparable clinical systems leave similar questions open: Jiao and Kannampallil (2024) report intraoperative data-delivery latencies of several minutes, while Tonekaboni et al. (2022) report a p90 of 64.27 s for physiological streaming. By contrast, stream-processing benchmarks achieve tens to low hundreds of milliseconds (Henning et al. 2024). This gap motivates measuring the assembled clinical pipeline rather than assuming benchmark performance transfers to deployment.
This thesis asks:
RQ1. What latency floor does the streaming architecture for real-time hypotension prediction sustain at low load, and where does its operating boundary lie relative to the clinical timing reference?
RQ2. How do concurrent case count and inference demand affect pipeline latency and per-case progress under realistic clinical workloads, and what bottleneck emerges as the operating boundary is approached?
An instrumented replay-based testbed comprising Kafka, Flink, configurable inference and a clinician-facing interface is used to establish the low-load latency floor and then characterize behaviour as concurrent case count and inference demand increase. The result is an empirical operating region showing where the architecture remains stable and where degradation first arises.
2. Background
2.1 Intraoperative hypotension and the timing constraint
Predictive systems such as the Hypotension Prediction Index (HPI) can identify impending hypotension minutes in advance, but the nominal prediction horizon is not the same as the warning time available after an alert is raised. Rellum et al. (2025) measured this interval directly for correctly predicted events. Depending on the alerting rule, mean warning times ranged from 1.75 to 3.52 minutes; HPI-85 provided 2.39 minutes. Persistence requirements can also trade warning time for improved specificity. Infrastructure therefore consumes an interval already shared by alert confirmation, clinician response and treatment. Section 4 derives the timing reference used in the evaluation.
2.2 Stream-processing mechanisms
Physiological measurements are processed in event time so that windows correspond to when measurements occurred rather than when they arrived. Watermarks estimate event-time progress and determine when windowed results can be produced (Akidau et al. 2015).
More importantly, logical separation between patient streams does not imply performance isolation. Although keying by surgical case separates state, cases may still share Kafka partitions, Flink subtasks, buffers and downstream capacity. Uneven workloads can overload shared resources, uneven event-time progress can delay processing, and slow inference can propagate backpressure upstream (Carbone et al. 2015; Karimov et al. 2018; Akidau et al. 2021). Increasing concurrency or inference demand may therefore cause individual cases to fall behind despite being logically independent.
2.3 Reference platform
The architectural starting point is the peri-operative platform of Joachim et al. (2026), in which Philips monitor data passes through DataWarehouse Connect and Mirth Connect into Kafka and Flink before long-term storage. The platform has operated since 2019 across 77 operating-room and ICU beds and covered 118,915 patients. A typical patient contributes a median of six waveform and ten numeric channels; numerics are sampled at 1–5 Hz, waveforms at 100–200 Hz and ECG at 500 Hz. These figures provide both a reference architecture and an empirical workload envelope.
The platform was designed primarily for reliable data capture and retrospective use. Its evaluation reports coverage but not end-to-end latency or scaling under concurrent load, and the authors identify clinical decision support as future work. This thesis reproduces only the real-time path relevant to that transition — replayed physiological data, Kafka, Flink, inference and interface — and measures its operating boundary when timeliness becomes a requirement.
3. Related work
Clinical research demonstrates that real-time inference over physiological data is feasible but provides limited characterization of the infrastructure carrying it. Nagaraj et al. (2024) describe multi-bed inference over high-frequency physiological waveforms without characterizing latency or scaling under concurrent load. Tonekaboni et al. (2022) likewise consider multi-patient physiological streaming but extrapolate adequacy from single-stream measurements. Jiao and Kannampallil (2024) are an important exception: their deployed intraoperative ML pipeline shows mean EHR-to-model-ingestion delays of 2.72–3.26 minutes, demonstrating that substantial delay can arise around a deployed model.
Other clinical work addresses adjacent concerns: Corbin et al. (2023) study silent deployment and production monitoring, Wang et al. (2025) streaming validation, and Ospitia et al. (2026) inference-service scaling. LifeStream demonstrates operational problems caused by uneven progress between physiological streams (Jayarajan et al. 2021), while VitalDB (Lee et al. 2022) and Malunjkar et al. (2021) characterize real operating-room data and its quality limitations.
The systems literature supplies the measurement concepts needed here. Karimov et al. (2018) establish event-time latency methodology and demonstrate the effects of key skew, while Theodolite uses trends in processing lag to distinguish sustainable from unsustainable operation (Henning & Hasselbring 2021). ShuffleBench provides relevant measurements for keyed stateful dataflows (Henning et al. 2024); Horchidan et al. (2024) examine inference placement and scaling; Kalim et al. (2018) resource isolation and starvation; and Akidau et al. (2021) coupling through uneven event-time progress. Vogel et al. (2024) address fault recovery, a complementary next step once the stable operating region has been established.
The gap lies between these literatures. Clinical systems provide the workload but generally do not measure how performance changes as concurrent cases and inference demand increase; systems research provides the methods but has not applied them to concurrent perioperative waveform streams under a clinical timing constraint. This thesis connects the two. Its contributions are an empirical characterization of the architecture’s operating boundary, a per-case progress measure for exposing cross-case interference, and a clinical timing framework that can be updated when the TRANSFER prediction model becomes available.
4. Clinical latency reference
The infrastructure constraint is a function of four terms:
T_warning = T_confirm + T_pipeline + T_clinician + T_treatment
The values below are current estimates rather than fixed clinical constants.
T_warning — alert to hypotension — ~105–211 s. Rellum et al. (2025) report these mean intervals among correctly predicted events. HPI-85 gives 143 s and HPI-85 with 40-second persistence 155 s. HPI is used because the TRANSFER model is still under development.
T_confirm — alert confirmation — 0 or ~40 s. Persistence can improve specificity by filtering transient threshold crossings (Rellum et al. 2025); this time is therefore not infrastructure slack.
T_pipeline — acquisition, processing, inference and delivery — measured by this thesis.
T_clinician — alert to clinical action — unknown. No suitable estimate was identified in the reviewed literature.
T_treatment — physiological response to treatment — provisionally ~42 s. Schwinn and Reves (1989) report approximately 42 s to maximal haemodynamic effect for phenylephrine. This is illustrative and treatment-specific; agents such as norepinephrine and ephedrine have different response profiles.
Thus,
T_pipeline + T_clinician = T_warning − T_confirm − T_treatment
currently leaves approximately 60–170 seconds for technical delivery and clinician response combined, depending on the alert definition. Because clinician response is unknown, this is an upper bound rather than a service-level objective. For context, Jiao and Kannampallil (2024) measured EHR-to-model-ingestion delays of approximately 163–196 seconds.
The relationship matters more than any individual number. The current values provide a provisional reference against which the measured operating boundary can be interpreted; once the TRANSFER model and intended clinical intervention are known, the calculation can be updated.
5. Approach: the testbed
The testbed builds on infrastructure and data sources already available at Charité. The LIVIA project provides a replay mechanism that reads recorded DataWarehouse Connect (DWC) Parquet files and streams physiological measurements into Kafka. This thesis uses that existing replay path rather than implementing a new data generator. Additional case-level information, including demographic variables such as age, height and weight, will initially be provided from CORR as downloaded files.
The contribution of this thesis begins downstream of these existing inputs. The existing data path is extended into a real-time decision-support pipeline in which Kafka streams are processed in Flink, prepared for inference and delivered to the TRANSFER interface. Cases remain keyed by surgical case so that multiple recorded operations can be replayed concurrently while their processing state and progress remain separately observable. CORR data provide case-level enrichment where required, while DWC physiological measurements constitute the high-frequency streaming workload.
Because the TRANSFER hypotension model is still under development, the thesis implements a configurable external inference stub with controllable service time, latency variability and worker capacity. Flink invokes the service asynchronously, allowing inference demand to be varied independently of case concurrency and, at saturation, to produce queueing and backpressure (Horchidan et al. 2024). The unknown serving cost of the future prediction model therefore becomes an experimental variable rather than an assumption.
A central contribution is an end-to-end latency benchmark around this pipeline. The benchmark records when data enters the experimental pipeline and when the corresponding completed prediction reaches the interface; their difference defines pipeline latency. Source measurement timestamps additionally permit event-time staleness to be calculated. Kafka and Flink lag, inference service and queueing time, and resource utilisation are collected to identify where delay accumulates. The replay mechanism operates open-loop so that pipeline slowdown does not reduce the offered workload and conceal queueing delay, following established stream-processing benchmarking practice (Karimov et al. 2018; Henning et al. 2024).
The testbed additionally derives per-case event-time progress lag. Aggregate latency can show that the pipeline is degrading, but not whether all concurrent cases degrade equally. Tracking event-time progress separately for each case makes cross-case interference observable when cases share Kafka, Flink or inference resources. Before the main experiments, the replay path, broker and Flink source/sink configuration are calibrated to ensure that an accidental testbed bottleneck is not mistaken for the operating boundary under study.
6. Evaluation plan
The evaluation locates the pipeline’s operating boundary across two dimensions: concurrent surgical cases and inference demand.
Three headline metrics are used. Pipeline latency (T_pipeline) measures wall-clock time from testbed ingress to receipt by the interface. Event-time staleness measures the age of the newest physiological evidence contributing to a prediction at delivery. Per-case progress lag measures how far each case falls behind its source event time and therefore exposes cross-case interference.
Following Theodolite (Henning & Hasselbring 2021), stability is determined from the trend in lag over a fixed observation interval. A configuration is stable when the regression slope of per-case progress lag remains below a predefined tolerance and degrading when lag exhibits sustained positive growth. The tolerance and observation interval are fixed during calibration. Kafka/Flink lag, inference queueing and service time, and resource utilisation are used to localize bottlenecks; latency is reported using percentiles rather than means alone (Karimov et al. 2018).
The evaluation proceeds in two stages. First, inference is held at a low fixed cost while simultaneous case count increases from a single-case baseline. The per-case workload is parameterized against Joachim et al. (2026): approximately six waveform and ten numeric channels, with waveforms at 100–200 Hz, ECG at 500 Hz and numerics at 1–5 Hz. This establishes the low-load latency floor and measures how latency, staleness and per-case progress change as cases increasingly share infrastructure.
Second, at representative case counts, inference service time, variability and worker capacity are varied through saturation. This identifies the combinations of concurrency and inference demand at which queueing and backpressure make progress unstable and latency increase.
Before performance experiments, outputs are checked against a reference implementation and the harness, Kafka and Flink configuration are calibrated to avoid accidental bottlenecks. The resulting output is an operating region over case count and inference demand, together with its measured latency relative to the provisional clinical reference.
7. Threats to validity and workplan
7.1 Threats to validity
Replay is not live deployment. Recorded data preserve physiological content and measurement timing but may not preserve arrival behaviour such as network jitter, batching or transport delay. Receipt timestamps in the available DWC data will therefore be checked before implementation; if absent, no claim is made to reproduce those dynamics.
Inference is synthetic. The configurable stub characterizes the pipeline as a function of inference demand rather than measuring the final TRANSFER model directly. The real model can later be mapped onto the measured operating region if its serving characteristics fall within the evaluated range.
The clinical reference is provisional. HPI substitutes for the unfinished TRANSFER model, clinician response is unknown, and treatment time depends on the intervention. Section 4 therefore provides an interpretive reference rather than a clinical service-level objective.
The testbed is incomplete. Integration and storage components are omitted, and the workload profile derives primarily from one perioperative platform and monitor family. Results therefore characterize the evaluated real-time path rather than a complete production system or all clinical environments.
7.2 Workplan and risks
The thesis targets sixteen weeks within a six-month completion window, with writing proceeding alongside implementation.
Weeks 1–2: confirm DWC access and timestamps, treatment assumptions and available recordings; validate and configure the LIVIA replay mechanism.
Weeks 1–6: build and instrument the Kafka/Flink, inference and interface path, integrating the existing LIVIA replay mechanism and required CORR case-level data.
Weeks 7–8: validate single-case correctness, calibrate the stability criterion and verify that the testbed itself is not the bottleneck.
Weeks 9–12: run the concurrency and inference-demand experiments.
Weeks 11–14: analyse the operating boundary and localize observed bottlenecks.
Weeks 14–16: complete results, discussion and conclusions.
The primary replay source is recorded Charité DataWarehouse Connect data from real perioperative cases. If access is delayed, VitalDB provides a fallback, with its different monitor technology and sampling characteristics reported as a limitation. If the existing TRANSFER interface is unavailable or cannot be integrated within the thesis timeframe, a minimal consumer will provide the same prediction-receipt measurement boundary. Schedule pressure is handled by reducing sweep resolution rather than expanding scope. A result in which no degradation occurs within the evaluated range remains informative because it establishes a measured operating region; fault-recovery experiments can subsequently build on that baseline. Ethics approval covering this work is already in place through the Charité ethics committee.
